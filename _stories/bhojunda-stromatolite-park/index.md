Bhojunda Stromatolite Park sits just southwest of Chittaurgarh, in Rajasthan, a small protected patch of rock declared a National Geological Monument in 1976. It does not look dramatic, but the low, layered domes in the limestone here are ancient fossils of microbial life: stromatolites built roughly 1.6 billion years ago.

A stromatolite is not a shell or a bone. It is a structure built by microbial mats, mostly cyanobacteria, the simple organisms that live in sheets on the floor of shallow, sunlit seas. The mats are sticky, and they work in a slow cycle: they trap and bind fine grains of sediment drifting past, while minerals precipitating from the water help cement each layer. As sediment buries the mat, the microbes grow upward toward the light and build another layer. Do this for years and the layers stack into the domes and columns you see in the rock, each one preserving a surface once shaped by microbial life.

Why does that matter? Because microbial life like this was among the earliest life on Earth: stromatolites of this kind stretch back around 3.5 billion years, long before anything with a skeleton existed. The cyanobacteria that build them also do something world-changing. They photosynthesise, releasing oxygen as a by-product, and over geological time photosynthetic microbes like these drove up the amount of oxygen in the air, reshaping the planet's environment and eventually making complex, oxygen-breathing life possible. For much of Earth's early history, microbial communities were the dominant form of life in many environments.

The Bhojunda stromatolites formed in a shallow marine setting in the Proterozoic, and are now preserved in a rock unit called the Bhagwanpura Limestone. Their exact age is not pinned down, since the dating of these Vindhyan rocks is debated, but they are generally placed around 1.6 billion years old, in the Mesoproterozoic. Several different shapes of stromatolite occur at the site, reflecting different forms of stromatolite growth.

So a quiet roadside outcrop near Chittaurgarh is really a window onto the deep past: fossilised structures made by ancient microbial life, the same kind of photosynthetic microbes that helped fill the air with oxygen. It is also fragile, and geologists have flagged it as a site in real need of protection.
