The Nilgiris are a high, steep-sided plateau where Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Kerala meet, climbing to about 2,637 m at Doddabetta. They rise so sharply from the plains on every side that the tableland on top feels like an island floating in the sky.

The rock they are built from carries an astonishing history. It is mostly charnockite and granulite gneiss, and both belong to the deep crust. Granulite is rock that was buried far down inside the Earth, roughly 25 to 35 kilometres below the surface, and baked there at intense heat and pressure until its minerals reset into a hard, dry, coarse-grained stone. The Nilgiri rock formed and was cooked in Neoarchean time, with an age of about 2,550 million years and its deep baking around 2,450 million years ago, which makes it some of the oldest lower crust exposed anywhere in India. What you walk on at the summit is stone that was once tens of kilometres underground.

For that buried rock to stand as a mountain, two things had to happen: it had to be lifted, and everything above it had to be stripped away. The lifting came through block-faulting. The Nilgiri block is a slab of crust bounded by ancient fracture belts, the Moyar and Bhavani shear zones along its northern side and the broad low corridor of the Palghat Gap to its south. Along these old lines of weakness the whole block was pushed up as a unit during the more recent Cenozoic era, which is why its edges are cliffs rather than gentle slopes. The block went up largely in one piece, so its rim is sharp all the way around.

This uplift is part of the same story that made the Western Ghats. When India tore away from Madagascar around 88 to 90 million years ago, the crust along the new western coast was heaved up into a raised rim, and later erosion and slow isostatic rebound, the crust floating upward as rivers carried its weight out to sea, kept renewing the height. As the land rose, running water wore down the softer cover and carried it off, uncovering the tough deep-crust rock beneath. The minerals in the Nilgiri charnockite even preserve textures that point to fairly rapid uplift and cooling, a record of that long journey from deep underground to open air.

The result stands out for both geography and life. The Nilgiris are the knot where the Western and Eastern Ghats meet, a high hub joining the two mountain chains. Their height and steep isolation give them a cool, wet climate cut off from the hot plains below, because the plateau intercepts the monsoon and its summit weather is closer to a temperate hill country than to South India. That island-like separation let a distinctive montane flora evolve here, and it is protected as the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, India's first. The place is high, cool and unusual for the same reason it is old: a block of the planet's early deep crust, lifted whole and laid bare.
