Vembanad is a long, narrow sheet of water lying just inland of the Arabian Sea, running for close to 100 kilometres down the Kerala coast behind a low ridge of sand. It is the longest lake in India and the heart of the region's famous backwaters, the network of calm channels that houseboats drift through.

It is not a lake in the usual sense of a rain-fed basin in the hills. It is a lagoon: a strip of coastal water walled off from the open sea by a sand barrier, kept brackish where salt water and river water mix. Kerala's coast is lined with a chain of these lagoons, and Vembanad is the largest of them.

To see how it formed, it helps to picture this coast a few thousand years ago. During the Holocene, the warm stretch since the last ice age, sea level rose and then settled, and the low ground behind the shore flooded into a shallow bay of the Arabian Sea. Studies of the peat and shell layers under the lake record this back and forth, a rise around 8,000 to 6,000 years ago followed by a fall a few thousand years later, so the shoreline advanced and retreated more than once before the present arrangement locked in.

The barrier that finally closed the bay off was built by the sea itself. Six rivers run down from the Western Ghats and drop their loads of sand and mud where they meet the coast, and waves and the currents that sweep sand along the shore (longshore drift) pile that sediment into a long bar just offshore. Over time the bar grew and joined up, stretching roughly from Kayamkulam in the south to Kochi in the north, penning the water behind it. What had been an open bay became a lagoon with only narrow openings to the sea.

Because the lagoon floor sat low and rivers kept feeding it, parts of the surrounding land ended up below the level of the sea. This is Kuttanad, where farmers have grown rice on reclaimed lake bed, some 1.5 to 2 metres below sea level, for around two centuries, one of the few places on Earth where paddy is worked below the tide. To protect that crop, the Thanneermukkom barrage was thrown across the lake in 1976. It shuts during the dry season to hold back salty water pushing in from the sea, splitting the lake into a fresh southern half and a salty northern one so the fields stay sweet while the rice grows.

That same barrage shows the tension the lake now lives with. It saves the harvest, but by blocking the daily exchange with the sea it also stops fish and prawns migrating upstream, lets weeds choke the fresh water, and slows the natural flushing that once carried pollution out. Vembanad is a Ramsar site of international importance, valued for its birds, its fisheries and its farmland, and all of that rests on the same simple fact of its birth: a bay that rivers and waves sealed behind a bar of sand, leaving a long, shallow, half-fresh, half-salt water body that people have leaned on ever since.
