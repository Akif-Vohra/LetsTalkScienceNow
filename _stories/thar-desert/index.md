The Thar, or Great Indian Desert, is the sandy, wind-built desert of northwestern India, spread mostly across Rajasthan and continuing over the border into Pakistan. It runs roughly 800 kilometres long and 490 kilometres wide, a landscape of rolling sand hills, low dunes and scrubby plains, bounded to the south by the salt flats of the Rann of Kutch and to the west by the plain of the Indus.

What makes it a desert is not distance from the sea, because the Arabian Sea is close by, but position and timing. The Thar sits around 25 to 28 degrees north, the same subtropical band that holds many of the world's great deserts, and it lies at the far northwestern edge of the Indian summer monsoon. The rains that soak the rest of the country from June to September have mostly spent themselves by the time they reach this corner, so only about 12 to 25 centimetres of rain fall here in a year, nearly all of it in those few monsoon months.

"Aeolian" simply means shaped by wind. The sand is mostly quartz, weathered out of older rock and washed down by streams, then picked up by the wind and heaped into dunes. Loose sand moves easily, so the dunes shift and change shape rather than staying put.

The most surprising thing about the Thar is that it breathes with the monsoon. Geologists dug into an 18-metre dune and dated its layers, reading back roughly the last 190,000 years, and found it was never one long dry spell. The sand piled up in pulses, separated by calmer, moister spells when grass and even woodland took hold. Each pulse tracks the monsoon growing weaker or stronger: when the rains pulled back the sand advanced, and when they returned the desert greened.

Those wetter times left their trace in vanished rivers. Buried under the sand run the old channels of the Ghaggar-Hakra system. These were rivers fed by a strong monsoon, not by Himalayan meltwater, and as the monsoon weakened over the last few thousand years they shrank from year-round to seasonal and were largely gone by about 4,000 years ago, which helped unsettle the farming communities living along their banks. Today the Luni is the only sizeable river that still crosses the desert.

What little water remains tells the same story. In shallow basins with no outlet to the sea, the water that collects can only leave by evaporating, and it leaves its dissolved salt behind, building salt lakes such as Sambhar. Much of the groundwater beneath is old and brackish. So the Thar is not a dead sea of sand but a living desert balanced on the edge of the monsoon, one that has been greener in the past and could be again.
