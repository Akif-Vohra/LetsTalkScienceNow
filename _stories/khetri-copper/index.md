The Khetri Copper Belt is a long, narrow strip of copper-bearing rock in the hills of northeastern Rajasthan, in Jhunjhunu district, running for more than a hundred kilometres along the northern part of the Aravalli-Delhi mountain belt. It is the classic copper district of India, and copper is still mined here today.

The copper is locked in chalcopyrite, a brass-coloured sulphide of copper and iron. It does not sit evenly through the rock but is gathered into lodes, and the interesting question is how it came to be concentrated in those particular places. The short answer is hot, salty water. During the long building of the mountains here, heated underground brines moved through the deep rock, dissolving copper and iron out of a large volume of it and carrying them along. Where the chemistry or the pressure shifted, especially along cracks and shear zones, the metals came out of solution and built up as ore.

Deposits made this way are rich in iron oxides and copper (with a little gold), and the rock around them carries an early flush of sodium-rich alteration left by the fluids. Geologists call this family iron-oxide-copper-gold, or IOCG, deposits, and Khetri is one of the better-known examples. The rock that hosts the ore began as ordinary sediment laid down in a shallow Proterozoic sea, later buried, cooked into schists and quartzites, and folded during mountain-building.

Working out when the copper actually formed took careful dating. Tiny crystals of a mineral called monazite grew as the ore-forming fluids passed through, and their uranium-lead clock reads about 835 million years, so the metal was introduced late in the Proterozoic, long after the host sediments were laid down. As with many deposits of this kind, the finer points of how and exactly when it formed are still debated.

People have drawn copper from this belt since antiquity, and it remains one of India's most important copper-mining regions, still worked today. The ore that rewards all that effort is, in the end, a record of ancient hot water moving through deep rock and leaving its metal behind.
