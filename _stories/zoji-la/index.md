Zoji La is a pass at about 3,500 m, a notch in the crest of the Great Himalaya that lets a route cross from the green Kashmir Valley into the dry country of Ladakh.

A pass is just the lowest place to get over a mountain wall. Zoji La is a dip in the highest continuous ridge of the western Himalaya, between Sonamarg on the western side and Dras to the east. The wall itself was raised by the collision of India and Asia, the same slow continental pileup that built the whole range. Where a river cut back into the crest, or a glacier gouged out a saddle, the ridge was worn a little lower, and that low point became the crossing.

A high ridge does more than block a road; it blocks weather, and that is what makes this pass interesting. Moist air moving in from the west is forced to climb over the Great Himalayan wall. Rising air cools, and cool air cannot hold as much water vapour as warm air, so it drops its moisture as rain and snow on the western, windward slopes. By the time that air spills over the crest and sinks toward Ladakh, it has been wrung nearly dry. This is the **rain-shadow effect**, and Zoji La sits right on its hinge.

That is why the two sides look like different worlds within a few kilometres of each other. West of the pass, around Sonamarg, the slopes are forested and fed by heavy snow; east, toward Dras and Ladakh, the land is cold high desert. The crossing is roughly the same height on either side, so the difference is not altitude. It is which side of the moisture barrier you happen to stand on.

Zoji La is snowbound for much of the year precisely because it catches that western snowfall head-on, and a tunnel is being driven beneath it to keep the route open through winter. It is two things at once: a low point in the mountains and a high threshold for weather, a notch worn into a collision-built wall that happens to mark the line where the wet mountains end and the dry plateau begins.
