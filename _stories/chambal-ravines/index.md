The Chambal ravines are a vast, broken country of steep gullies cut into the land along the Chambal river, where the borders of Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh meet. Branching back from the river for kilometres in a dense maze of sharp ridges and deep channels, they form one of the largest stretches of badland in India. "Badland" is simply the geologist's term for ground so chewed up by erosion that it is hard to cross and hard to farm.

The starting material matters. The land here is thick, soft alluvium: silt and clay dropped by rivers over ages, in places more than a hundred metres deep. It is loosely packed, easily washed away, and holds little binding vegetation, which makes it about as vulnerable to running water as ground gets.

Ravines grow through gully erosion, and it works like this. Rain falls on the bare, soft ground and the runoff scratches shallow channels into it. Each channel gathers more water and so cuts deeper, into a gully. A gully does not only deepen; it eats backwards, up-slope and away from the river, and throws off side branches as it goes. Repeat that across a whole valley over thousands of years and you get the Chambal's maze.

What set all this cutting going is the river itself, through an idea geologists call base level. A stream can only wear down to the level of whatever it empties into, and for these gullies that floor is the Chambal. When the Chambal deepened its own channel and cut down into the plain, it lowered that floor, so every small stream draining towards it suddenly had further to fall. They steepened and began biting back into the soft alluvium. The river's downcutting is linked to a stronger monsoon in the early Holocene, which gave the Chambal more power to carve, and possibly to gentle uplift of the land; how much each mattered is still debated.

Today the river threading through this rough country is protected as the National Chambal Sanctuary, and it runs unusually clean for a northern Indian river. It shelters the gharial, the slender-snouted fish-eating crocodile, along with the Ganges river dolphin and freshwater turtles. The ravines themselves, hard to police and easy to vanish into, were for generations the refuge of outlaws.
