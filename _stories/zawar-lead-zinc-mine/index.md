Zawar is an old mining district in the hills south of Udaipur, in Rajasthan, where zinc and lead ore has been dug from ancient rock for more than two thousand years. It is best known for one achievement: this is where people first made metallic zinc on an industrial scale, long before anyone in Europe managed it.

The ore itself is very old. The metals sit in Palaeoproterozoic dolomite, a magnesium-rich cousin of limestone, as two sulphide minerals: sphalerite, which carries the zinc, and galena, which carries the lead and a little silver. Most geologists read the deposit as an ancient seafloor accumulation, where metal-rich fluids seeped into a shallow Proterozoic sea and the metals settled into the muds that later hardened into dolomite. Lead-isotope model ages put the metal at roughly 1,700 million years old, though exactly how and when it formed is still debated.

Zinc is a genuinely awkward metal to make, which is what makes Zawar's story remarkable. With copper, lead or iron you heat the ore in a furnace and the molten metal pools at the bottom. Zinc will not cooperate. To free zinc from its ore you have to heat it past about 900 degrees Celsius, but zinc also boils at almost exactly that temperature, so the metal comes off as a vapour. In an open furnace that vapour meets the air and instantly burns back into a white powder of zinc oxide. The metal escapes up the chimney before you can catch it.

The answer found at Zawar was distillation. The ore was sealed inside small clay retorts, each about the size of a bottle, and heated hard. The zinc vapour was led downward through a tube, away from the air, into a cooler space where it condensed back into liquid metal that could be collected. Dozens of these retorts were stacked over a single furnace and run together, turning a delicate trick into a production line. This downward distillation is, as far as we know, the first zinc smelting anywhere carried out at an industrial scale.

The timing is worth separating out. Ore has been mined at Zawar since about the fifth century BCE, at first mainly for lead and silver. The distillation of zinc metal came much later: radiocarbon dates from the smelting debris reach back to around the ninth century CE, and zinc production boomed here from the fourteenth to the sixteenth centuries before dying out in the early 1800s. Europe did not work out the same process until the eighteenth century, several hundred years behind.

Mining never really stopped, and Zawar is a working zinc mine to this day. Scattered across the old workings, though, are mounds of spent clay retorts, the discarded hardware of that early industry, which is why the site is protected as part of India's geological heritage.
