Sambhar is the largest inland salt lake in India, a broad, shallow salt flat lying in a closed basin among the low hills on the eastern edge of the Thar, in Rajasthan. When the monsoon fills it, water can spread across about 230 square kilometres; for much of the year it is a glaring white crust of salt.

Why is a lake so far from any ocean this salty? The key is that it sits in a closed basin: the streams that feed it have nowhere to drain onward, so no water leaves except by evaporating into the dry desert air. Every stream carries a trace of dissolved salt, weathered out of the rocks it crossed. The water floats off as vapour, but the salt cannot, so it stays behind. Do that for thousands of years and the leftover salt builds and builds. It works like a pan of lightly salted water left on the stove: as the water boils away, what is left only grows saltier.

The salt here did not come from an ancient sea. Chemical and isotopic study of the lake shows its water is ordinary rainwater, and its salts were dissolved from the surrounding land and then concentrated in place. Cores drilled through the lakebed record more than 30,000 years of this slow build-up, with the mineral layers tracking how the brine's chemistry changed over time.

The lake breathes with the seasons. The summer monsoon, roughly June to September, floods the flats with a thin sheet of water; then through the long dry months it evaporates away and leaves fresh crusts of salt on the bed. What crystallises out is mostly halite, ordinary table salt, along with gypsum and other minerals that come out of solution as the brine grows more concentrated.

People have turned that rhythm into a harvest. Salt has been gathered at Sambhar for more than a thousand years, and today an embankment divides the lake, with the eastern side split into shallow ponds where the brine is left to dry down into salt.

The same shallow, salty water makes Sambhar a crucial refuge for birds. It is a protected Ramsar wetland, and when the monsoon briefly floods it, tens of thousands of flamingos and other migratory birds arrive to feed.
