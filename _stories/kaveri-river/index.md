The Kaveri rises high in the Western Ghats at Talakaveri, in the Kodagu hills of Karnataka, and flows east and south across the Deccan to reach the Bay of Bengal in Tamil Nadu.

Along the way it drops over rocky steps in noisy falls, wraps around river islands, and finally spreads into one of the oldest continuously farmed deltas on Earth. It is the great river of the south, and for two thousand years its delta has been the rice bowl of Tamil Nadu.

Why does it flow east at all, when its source sits within sight of the west coast? The answer is the shape of the land underneath it. Peninsular India is tilted. Its western edge, the Western Ghats, stands high, around 1.5 kilometres, and the whole block leans gently down toward the east. Water runs downhill, so the big peninsular rivers, the Godavari, the Krishna and the Kaveri among them, all drain that tilt eastward into the Bay of Bengal rather than the short, steep way west into the Arabian Sea.

The rock it crosses is some of the oldest on the planet. Most of the Kaveri's course runs over the Dharwar craton, an ancient, rigid block of crust made of granite, gneiss and greenstone that has been solid since Archaean and Proterozoic time, well over two billion years ago. This is hard, unyielding stone. Where the river meets a band of especially resistant rock, it cannot cut down through it quickly, so the water piles up and then tumbles over the edge. That is what makes the falls at Shivanasamudra and the gorge at Hogenakkal: they are places where the ancient crust refuses to wear away as fast as the softer ground around it, leaving a step in the riverbed. Geologists read these steps, along with the river's sharp, angled bends, as signs that the land here was uplifted again fairly recently, rejuvenating an old, ancestral river and giving it fresh energy to carve.

Downstream, everything changes. As the Kaveri nears the coast the hard rock gives way to a deep pile of soft sediment. The whole southeast corner of India sits on the Cauvery basin, a great crack in the crust that opened during the Cretaceous, more than 100 million years ago, as India tore away from Sri Lanka and Antarctica in the breakup of the supercontinent Gondwana. The stretching thinned and dropped the crust here, making a long-lived hollow that has been collecting river mud and sand ever since. So the river arrives at a place already built to receive its sediment.

The delta itself is young. For thousands of years the Kaveri has been dumping its load where it meets the sea, and the coastline has been growing outward into the Bay of Bengal at roughly ten kilometres every thousand years. After the last ice age, when melting ice pushed the sea inland around 9,000 to 6,000 years ago and then steadied, the river went to work building the flat, fertile plain we see now. Old beach ridges preserved in the delta trace where the shoreline used to be, a record of the sea advancing and the river slowly winning back the land.

That long history is why the river looks and behaves the way it does. Upstream, over the stubborn old craton, it is a river of waterfalls and rocky islands, cutting slowly into some of the toughest crust anywhere. Downstream, over the sinking, sediment-filled basin, it is a broad, calm, muddy river that fans across a delta of its own making. People noticed the value of that delta early: around the second century, engineers built the Grand Anicut, or Kallanai, a low stone barrage across the river to spread its water over the fields. It still works today, one of the oldest functioning water-control structures in the world, and the delta it feeds remains the heartland of Tamil rice farming, exactly as it was for the Chola kings who depended on it.
