Ramgarh is a near-circular ring of hills about 3.5 to 4 kilometres across, standing in the Vindhyan sandstone country of southeastern Rajasthan, with a village and an old temple sitting inside the bowl. For a long time it was read as just an oddly round set of hills. It is now confirmed as a meteorite impact structure, India's third, alongside [Lonar](/stories/lonar-crater/) and the much older [Dhala](/stories/dhala-impact-structure/).

How do you tell a circular hill from a real crater? Roundness alone is not enough, since ordinary geology can make rings too. The proof lies in what an impact does to rock at the instant of collision. An asteroid arrives at several kilometres per second and drives a shock wave through the ground far more violent than anything everyday earth processes produce, and that shock leaves fingerprints. At Ramgarh geologists found shatter cones, the nested cone shapes that rock splinters into under a passing shock front, and planar deformation features, sets of microscopic parallel lines inside quartz grains that form only under the sudden, immense pressure of an impact. Nothing but an impact is known to make them.

The shape of the structure holds a second surprise: that ring of raised sandstone is not the rim of the crater. When a large body strikes, the ground beneath the impact rebounds, much as water rebounds into a peak after a stone drops in, and pushes up a central uplift. A 2020 study showed that Ramgarh's visible ring is the eroded remnant of exactly that central uplift, and that the full crater was larger, roughly 10 kilometres across. Erosion has since worn away the outer parts and left the tougher core standing.

When did it happen? That is honestly still unsettled. The rock the asteroid hit is Neoproterozoic Vindhyan sandstone, so the impact has to be younger than that. Recent uranium-lead dating of shock-damaged zircon crystals points to somewhere around 530 to 395 million years ago, though other studies have argued for quite different ages, and pinning down the date is ongoing work.

Today Ramgarh is protected and being developed as a geo-heritage site. Inside the crater bowl stands the tenth-century Bhand Devra temple, sometimes called a "mini Khajuraho," so a scar left by a visitor from space now shelters a thousand-year-old piece of human history.
