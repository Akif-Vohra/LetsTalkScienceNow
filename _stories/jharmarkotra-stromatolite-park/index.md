Jharmar Kotra (usually spelled Jhamarkotra) sits just southeast of Udaipur, in Rajasthan, where Palaeoproterozoic rocks preserve stromatolites beside a large rock-phosphate mine. What makes the site unusual is not only the stromatolites but what they are made of: this is one of the oldest large phosphate deposits on Earth.

Stromatolites are layered structures built by mats of microbes in shallow seas, the same kind of ancient life recorded at [Bhojunda](/stories/bhojunda-stromatolite-park/). At Jhamarkotra they take columnar and branching forms, mounds that grew layer by layer on a Proterozoic seafloor.

The twist is the phosphate. Most sedimentary rock is built of silica or of lime (calcium carbonate), but the rock here is instead rich in phosphate, in the mineral apatite, the same material that makes up bones and teeth and modern fertiliser. Phosphorus is normally a scarce nutrient in seawater, so rock this loaded with it needs explaining, and the explanation is the microbes. The mats scavenged and stored phosphorus, and within them the conditions let calcium phosphate settle out, building stromatolites made partly of phosphate. The microbial communities did not just pile up mounds; they concentrated a scarce element into what is now an ore.

That makes Jhamarkotra important twice over. It is among the world's oldest significant sedimentary phosphate deposits, laid down in the Palaeoproterozoic (its exact age is debated, but very roughly two billion years ago), and the same rocks preserve signs of early photosynthetic microbes, the cyanobacteria that were beginning to add oxygen to the young Earth's air.

Today that ancient chemistry is put to work: Jhamarkotra is India's main source of rock phosphate, dug for fertiliser, and the fossil park preserves the stromatolite mounds in the rock alongside the workings. A structure built by microbes some two billion years ago now helps feed modern crops.
