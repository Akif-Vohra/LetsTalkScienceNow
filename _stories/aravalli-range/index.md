The Aravalli Range is the long belt of ancient hills that runs across northwestern India, stretching roughly 700 kilometres from Gujarat, through Rajasthan, towards Delhi. Despite the word "range," it is not tall: its highest point, Guru Shikhar on Mount Abu, reaches only about 1,722 metres, and most of it is a chain of low, rounded ridges. That modest height is the interesting part, because these were once great mountains.

A mountain range is really a balance between two opposing forces. Tectonic movement pushes crust upward and builds the range, while erosion by rain, rivers and wind wears it back down. A range stays high only while the pushing keeps pace with the wearing. The Aravallis are low today because the pushing stopped long ago while the wearing never did.

They were built deep in the Proterozoic, very roughly two to one billion years ago, on top of even older Archaean rock (the Banded Gneissic Complex, more than three billion years old). Over that long span the crust here was first rifted apart and then squeezed back together in a series of collisions, the Aravalli and later Delhi mountain-building episodes, which crumpled and welded it into a high belt of folded rock.

Since that last mountain-building ended, around a billion years ago, this corner of India has been tectonically quiet. No fresh uplift has come to renew the peaks, but erosion has carried on without pause. Hundreds of millions of years of it have stripped kilometres of rock off the top. So what you stand on today is not the old summits but the deeply eroded root of the range: rock that once lay deep inside the mountains, now laid bare at the surface. It is the mirror image of the Himalaya, which stand tall because India is still colliding with Asia and pushing them up right now.

This great age is why the Aravallis are often called India's oldest mountains, and among the oldest on Earth. The rocks and the mountain-building really are ancient, though "the world's oldest range" is a slippery title that depends on exactly what you choose to count.

The old range still shapes the region in two ways. Its deep, ancient rocks are the source of the metals the area is famous for: the zinc and lead of Zawar and Rajpura-Dariba, the copper of Khetri, and the marble, all locked in Aravalli rock. And the ridge still works as a natural wall along the desert's edge, helping to check the eastward creep of the Thar's sand.
